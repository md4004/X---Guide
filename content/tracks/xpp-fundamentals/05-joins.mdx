<Step id="two-buffers">


Reading a header table and then querying its lines inside the loop costs one query per
header. X++ joins in the `select` itself, so it costs one:

<Snippet lang="xpp">{`SalesTable salesTable;
SalesLine salesLine;

while select salesTable
    join salesLine
    where salesLine.SalesId == salesTable.SalesId
{
    info(strFmt("%1 / %2", salesTable.SalesId, salesLine.ItemId));
}`}</Snippet>

Both buffers are filled on every pass. The `where` after the joined table **is** the join
condition — X++ has no separate `on` keyword, which reads oddly for about a day and then
stops mattering.

Run it and open the SQL trace: one `SELECT` with an `INNER JOIN`, whatever the row count.

<Aside title="A missing join condition is silent">
  Leave the `where` off and you get a cross join — every row against every row — with no
  warning at all. When a joined query returns a suspiciously round and suspiciously large
  number of rows, check the join condition first.
</Aside>

</Step>

<Step id="four-kinds">


| Written          | Means                                                                            |
| ---------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `join`           | Inner join. Only rows that match on both sides.                                  |
| `outer join`     | Left outer. Every outer row, with the joined buffer empty when there is no match. |
| `exists join`    | Keep the outer row **if** a match exists. No fields fetched.                      |
| `notexists join` | Keep the outer row **if no** match exists. No fields fetched.                     |

`exists join` and `notexists join` are the two most useful and the two most often missed.
They answer "which customers have ordered?" and "which never have?" without fetching a
single sales-order field — and, crucially, without duplicating the outer row once per
match:

<Snippet lang="xpp">{`CustTable custTable;
SalesTable salesTable;

while select custTable
    exists join salesTable
    where salesTable.CustAccount == custTable.AccountNum
{
    info(custTable.AccountNum);
}`}</Snippet>

A customer with six orders appears once. Write that as a plain `join` and they appear six
times — a bug nobody notices until someone sums a column.

</Step>

<Step id="customers-without-orders">

## Your turn

Now the opposite. Log the `AccountNum` of every customer with no sales orders at all, then
log:

There is a keyword for this. Do not collect the matches and subtract them yourself.

</Step>

<Step id="reading-the-trace">


Open the SQL trace on the last two tasks. `exists join` compiled to an
`EXISTS (SELECT 1 ...)` subquery rather than a join — which is how F&O compiles it, and
precisely why it cannot duplicate outer rows.

That is the habit worth taking from this lesson: after any query you are unsure about, look
at what it actually generated. The trace is not debug output, it is the answer.

</Step>
